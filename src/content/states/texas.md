Rural Texas is several states sharing a border. A Panhandle wheat farm, a Hill Country cedar brake, an East Texas pine lease, a South Texas colonia, and a Trans-Pecos ranch do not have the same internet problem. Pick the technology that matches the land in front of the house, then check whether a cooperative or a state grant is about to change it.

## Five kinds of distance

The Panhandle and South Plains are flat, windy, and icy, with easy sky and exchanges that cover a huge grid of sections. West Texas and the Trans-Pecos add mesas, long private roads, and dust. The Hill Country looks busy on a weekend and is full of limestone ridges and Ashe juniper that stop radio waves. East Texas has trees, ice, and old telephone plant. South Texas brush country and border colonias have a history of missing infrastructure a few miles from a wired city. On the Gulf coast the issue is storms. In each region, town fiber stops where the houses thin out.

## Satellite internet

Satellite is the practical service when the ridge, the cedar, or the sheer mileage beats a wire. Low-earth-orbit service, including Starlink, keeps delay in the tens of milliseconds, so video calls and payment terminals work. Geostationary plans, where a provider still sells them, linger near 600 milliseconds. Downloads can look acceptable on a test while a call does not.

Starlink still needs a wide open view of the sky. Juniper that you can “see through” will block a low mount, just as it blocks fixed wireless. West of the Pecos the sky is rarely the issue. Wind, dust in the connectors, and a waitlist in a popular cell are. Residential service is tied to the address. Roam plans move with you and usually cost more. A Hill Country subdivision can be waitlisted while a ranch in the next county is not.

Do not budget from a price you remember. U.S. residential low-earth-orbit service has commonly landed somewhere around $80 to $150 a month, and the hardware is often a few hundred dollars, with real gaps by plan and by how busy the cell is. Confirm the number on the provider’s site for that address. If you order through the referral link on this page, check that the credit shows at checkout. A kit from a store is a different transaction.

## Fixed wireless and cellular

Fixed wireless is often the best buy when a tower has a clean shot to the house. Licensed links hold up better. Unlicensed 5 GHz struggles in cedar and pine even when you can glimpse the tower. A twenty- to thirty-foot mast is a normal requirement, not an upsell. Ask who climbs that tower when the access road is icy, and whether a fiber grant is already assigned to the address.

Cellular home internet is a real option near highways and towers, and a gamble on a county road. In the Permian Basin, oilfield traffic loads sites that look excellent for a phone. Along the border, a strong signal in town can be gone at the colonia or the ranch gate. Test for a week at the building, evenings included, before you cancel a stable line. Ask whether the gateway allows an external antenna.

## Cooperatives, poles, and fiber

Texas still has dozens of telephone cooperatives, and they are usually the people who will actually build to a section road. Guadalupe Valley Telephone Cooperative has pushed fiber through a lot of the Hill Country. Big Bend Telephone covers far West Texas exchanges that national carriers treat as an afterthought. Valley Telephone Cooperative serves a wide stretch of South Texas. In the east, Eastex and Peoples are the familiar local names. In the Panhandle, XIT and Five Area are the cooperatives members call. This is not a complete list. If a cooperative is on your telephone bill, start there and ask which construction season includes your location.

Copper from national carriers is a fading option. Many will not start a new DSL circuit even when the map still shows one. Cable in a ditch is also not service: splicing lags plowing by weeks or months.

Poles stall projects that are otherwise funded. The Broadband Development Office runs a pole replacement program because old, crowded, or missing poles block new lines. If a grant for your area seems stuck, the unglamorous reason is often a pole and a permission, not a lack of fiber in the warehouse.

## Ice, heat, hurricanes, dust

North and central Texas ice storms, and Panhandle blizzards, take overhead plant and power down together. The February 2021 freeze was a severe version of a pattern, not a one-off. A satellite terminal or a fixed-wireless radio keeps working only if the mount survives and you can power it. Use a battery for short outages and a generator if you cannot be dark for two days.

Summer kills gear too. Routers left in an attic or a metal barn overheat from May onward, so keep them in the living space. In West Texas, dust works into unsealed connectors. Use a drip loop and a boot. On the coast, assume fiber on poles will not be the first thing restored after a hurricane.

## Where to verify the official picture

The state office is the [Texas Broadband Development Office](https://comptroller.texas.gov/programs/broadband/) at the Comptroller of Public Accounts. It awards grants and loans, publishes the statewide plan, and keeps the maps providers and communities argue from. The office’s [broadband maps](https://comptroller.texas.gov/programs/broadband/outreach/maps/) include the Texas Broadband Development Map and a BEAD project view. The state’s BEAD final proposal was approved in December 2025. A shaded award area still has to become a drop at your door. Questions go to broadband@cpa.texas.gov or 833-383-9236.

Pair that with the FCC [National Broadband Map](https://broadbandmap.fcc.gov/). It records provider claims. If you cannot actually buy the service shown, file an availability challenge. Those challenges are how false “served” labels get off a road that still needs a grant.
