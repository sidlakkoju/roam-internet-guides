A Montana address can sit miles of gravel past the last place a provider will drive, with a ridge between the house and the nearest tower. Choose the option that can see the building, stay up in January, and still make sense if the cooperative lights a fiber drop later.

## What the ground does to a signal

Montana has about a million people across the fourth-largest state. The west is mountains and timber. Valleys such as the Flathead, Mission, Bitterroot, and Blackfoot are narrow, and a forested ridge blocks paths that look open on a highway map. The east is high plains, breaks, and ranch roads. Sky view is easy. Towers and fiber laterals are not. The Hi-Line, the Missouri breaks, and the country around Miles City, Baker, and Circle share long power runs and a backhaul hop that may be the only one for many miles.

The Continental Divide splits networks as well as weather, so an outage on one side says little about the other. Wildfire can take out a ridge-top radio and its power line together.

Tribal lands need a separate check. The Blackfeet, Crow, Northern Cheyenne, Fort Peck, Fort Belknap, Rocky Boy's, and Flathead reservations have their own roads and sometimes their own plans. Statewide maps mislabel trust land often enough that you should ask the tribal contact before you trust a commercial coverage tool.

## Satellite internet

Where nothing else can see the house, satellite is the working service. Low-earth-orbit service, including Starlink, keeps latency in the tens of milliseconds, enough for video calls and most ranch software. Geostationary services, where they are still sold, sit near 600 milliseconds. A speed test can look fine while a call feels broken.

Starlink needs a wide clear patch of sky. In western timber that means a roof, a gable, or a short mast, not a porch under the lodgepoles. On the plains the problems are wind and drifting snow, not trees. Capacity is sold by cell. One valley can be waitlisted while the next drainage is in stock. Roam plans are built to move and usually cost more. Residential service is tied to the address.

Prices move. U.S. residential low-earth-orbit service has often fallen roughly in the $80 to $150 a month range, with equipment commonly a few hundred dollars, depending on plan and cell load. That is not a quote. Read the figure on the provider’s site. If you use the referral link on this page, confirm the credit at checkout. Retailer kits follow different rules.

## Fixed wireless and cellular

Fixed-wireless operators still cover many valleys. A radio on the roof aims at a bench, a butte, or a grain elevator. Licensed links are steadier. Unlicensed gear near 5 GHz suffers from leaves, new metal roofs, and a neighbor’s radio. A mast of twenty or thirty feet is often the whole link. Ask who climbs the tower in January, and whether a fiber grant will replace the radio.

Carrier home gateways work near towns and the interstates, then fall off on bench roads. Test the building for a week, including evenings, before you cancel another line. One LTE site for a whole valley still slows down at dinner.

## Cooperatives, copper, and fiber

Telephone cooperatives are the builders most likely to reach a ranch. Mid-Rivers Communications, across a huge part of eastern and central Montana, has been plowing fiber to the premise and has told members it does not charge to leave copper. Single rural projects run hundreds of miles for a few hundred locations. Cable in the ditch is not a spliced drop. Ask which season.

Blackfoot Communications and 3 Rivers Communications have been replacing copper in western valleys, and Blackfoot has added middle-mile fiber so towns are less dependent on one route. Elsewhere, call Nemont in the northeast, Triangle on the north-central exchanges, Range in the east, or InterBel in the far northwest.

Long copper loops still carry a landline and a weak data signal, and wet cable makes them worse. National carriers have been retiring copper, so a map dot is not a new circuit. A firm co-op date is worth waiting for. A date that slips every time you call is not a reason to stay on a connection that cannot run the place.

## Winter and the install

Design for January. Ground blizzards bury a low dish and load rime on a mast. Mountain roofs shed snow slabs that take hardware off an eave. Chinook swings load ice, then drop it. Mount the terminal where you can clear it, out of the slide path. It heats itself and draws more power in storms, so a cabin inverter sized for lights can sag.

A battery on the router covers short outages. Multi-day storms need a generator. Wireless and cellular also die when a tower runs out of fuel. Satellite keeps working if you have power and sky.

Check obstructions at the mount, and again with leaves on if you surveyed in March. Enter the cable on the sheltered side with a drip loop, and ground the mast. On a rental, get written permission. Federal antenna rules limit some restrictions, but not every rented roof. On a seasonal road, order before it closes.

## Where to verify the official picture

The state office is the [ConnectMT Broadband Program](https://connectmt.mt.gov/) at the Department of Administration. It runs state grants and Montana’s Broadband Equity, Access, and Deployment work. The BEAD award is about $629 million, paid to providers building unserved and underserved locations, not as a voucher for a dish. Contact is connectmt@mt.gov or 406-444-4099. Use the site to see whether a funded build includes your area.

Then open the FCC [National Broadband Map](https://broadbandmap.fcc.gov/). It shows what providers report, not what you can buy at a useful speed. If it lists service you have been refused, file an availability challenge on that location. A false “served” label can keep later grant rounds off your road.
