Maine looks small on a national map and gets large as soon as you leave the interstate. Woods close over the road, peninsulas run for miles, islands keep their own schedules, and an ice storm will put a tree through the only wire you had. Pick the option that still works when the canopy is in leaf and the power has been out since yesterday.

## Woods, rock, and water

Maine is among the most forested states. That single fact explains more failed installs than distance does. A camp that sits a quarter mile off a paved road can have no line of sight in any direction that matters. Western mountains add ridges. Aroostook County adds long farm roads and thin exchanges. Washington County and the Downeast coast add granite, few through-routes, and towns national carriers have skipped. The North Woods are seasonal camps, private roads, and almost no commercial plant.

A peninsula can be twenty road miles from a tower two miles across the water. Islands depend on the ferry for every truck roll. Some of those towns have built their own fiber. Others are still waiting on a lateral that has to cross rock and water. Treat each town as its own case.

## Satellite internet

Satellite is what you install when the trees or the ferry calendar will not wait for a grant. Low-earth-orbit service, including Starlink, keeps latency in the tens of milliseconds, so calls and ordinary remote work are realistic. Geostationary service, if it is still sold at that address, sits near 600 milliseconds and is a poor fit for calls.

Starlink does not punch through spruce. On a wooded lot the dish needs a roof or a mast that clears the crowns, not a shorter cable run under the hardwoods. A March survey can fail in June. Islands and open ledges have sky, and their design case is wind plus freezing rain. Residential service is tied to an address and can be waitlisted cell by cell. Roam service is built to move and usually costs more.

Prices change by plan and by capacity. Residential low-earth-orbit service in the U.S. has often been in a rough band of about $80 to $150 a month, with hardware commonly a few hundred dollars. Read the current offer on the provider’s site. If you use this page’s referral link, confirm the credit before you pay. Retail boxes follow different rules.

## Fixed wireless and cellular

Fixed wireless still serves camps where a tower clears the canopy. Unlicensed links fade when the leaves come in, and again when ice loads the radio. A dry October path is not a January design. Ask who reaches the tower after an ice storm, and whether the road is already on a fiber build.

Cellular coverage changed when T-Mobile took over much of the former UScellular network rural Maine had relied on. A handset that worked two years ago is not a current test. Borrow a hotspot for a week at the house, evenings included, before you drop every other option. Deep woods and parts of Washington County still go to nothing between ridges. An external antenna helps only if the gateway allows one.

## Last mile, and the backbone that is not a last mile

The companies people actually order from are local or regional. Axiom has been building fiber town by town Downeast and on some islands, with rates set per community rather than as one statewide price. Consolidated Communications sells fiber under the Fidium name on a growing set of roads. GWI, Pioneer Broadband, UniTel, and Otelco show up in different exchanges. Spectrum is common on busier corridors and thin beyond them. A pedestal already on your road is a better long-term bet than a radio in the trees. If they say you are in the next phase, ask for the construction season in writing.

That is separate from the state backbone. The Maine Connectivity Authority’s MOOSE Net is open-access middle mile, roughly 530 miles across more than a hundred communities, backed in part by a federal grant. It gives last-mile providers a sturdier route. It is not a household plan you can order from the state. MCA reports that since 2021 it has improved or established connectivity for well over 100,000 homes and businesses, and that by late 2024 every location had service or a plan. For 2026 it has described using on the order of $48 million in BEAD funds for remaining locations under 100/20 Mbps, a set it has put around 22,000 locations. Those counts move as projects finish. They are not a promise that your camp is on this year’s splice list.

## Ice, mud, and power

Freezing rain is harder on gear than a deep dry snow. Ice entombs dishes, fixed-wireless radios, and the trees that then fall on the power line. Snow-melt heaters handle accumulation better than a solid ice shell. Mount the terminal where you can reach it without climbing a glazed roof, and clear it with something that will not gouge the face.

Nor’easters make multi-day outages normal. A battery covers a blink. A generator has to feed the dish as well as the router if you need to stay online through the storm. Mud season slips construction dates, because trenchers do not belong on a thawing camp road. Order island freight around the ferry, not around a street-address delivery estimate.

## Where to verify the official picture

The public agency is the [Maine Connectivity Authority](https://www.maineconnectivity.org/). Its site describes infrastructure programs, digital-opportunity work, and the [MOOSE Net middle mile](https://www.maineconnectivity.org/middle-mile). ConnectMaine now operates as part of that same effort, so you do not have to hunt a second office for the old grant programs. Use MCA’s pages to see whether a project area includes your town, then call the named provider for the address.

Finish with the FCC [National Broadband Map](https://broadbandmap.fcc.gov/). If it shows a provider that will not take your order, file an availability challenge. Maine’s remaining grants are aimed at locations the maps still call unserved or underserved. A wrong “served” dot keeps your road in the wrong pile.
