An internet connection afloat can help with weather downloads, family contact and work at anchor. A boat also presents challenges that a campsite does not: changing headings, salt spray, rigging shadows and a battery system shared with navigation equipment. Start with your cruising area and vessel layout before choosing a terminal.

The service examples here refer to Starlink, but the installation principles apply to other marine satellite systems. Prices vary; check the official site for hardware, subscriptions, installation accessories and ocean data charges. An inexpensive-looking land package may not cover the passage you intend to make.

## Match the plan to the waters you will cruise

Separate marina use, coastal cruising and offshore passages when comparing subscriptions. They can have different coverage and billing rules. A plan suitable at a dock should not be assumed to work beyond coastal limits, in another country's territorial waters or throughout an ocean crossing.

Read the [official ocean-use guidance](https://starlink.com/support/article/952e770f-570e-d984-5014-35ae2add51c7) alongside the terms shown in your account. Check whether your chosen service requires an ocean option or a maritime-oriented plan, which hardware is supported, and how movement is treated. Restrictions can depend on your market and subscription, so avoid planning around a universal distance limit quoted in an old review.

For offshore travel, understand any priority-data allowance, usage-based option and behavior when included data runs out. Decide who can enable paid data and how you will monitor it. Verify coverage along the whole route, including destination ports, rather than checking only the home marina.

## Treat underway use as a separate installation requirement

A vessel moves even at anchor. Swinging, rolling and pitching change the relationship between the terminal and nearby obstructions. Confirm that the equipment and plan support your intended operation, including actual travel underway. A stationary dockside connection is only the beginning of the test.

Use a mount designed for the terminal and marine environment. It must withstand vessel motion, vibration and the loads expected at its location. A portable stand left on deck can slide, tip or become a trip hazard. Secure hardware away from emergency access, boarding routes and lines handled under load.

Check the installation at the dock, then during a short local outing in safe conditions. Look for connection interruptions during turns and changes of heading. If the pattern follows vessel orientation, inspect the surrounding structure before blaming coverage. Avoid making installation adjustments while the boat is maneuvering.

## Find open sky without compromising deck work

On a sailboat, masts, booms, sails and rigging can block parts of the terminal's view. On a powerboat, radar arches, cabin roofs and other antennas can cause similar problems. Test the intended position with the obstruction tool and consider the boat's different headings, not just the view while tied alongside.

Height may improve visibility but complicates maintenance and increases structural demands. Choose a location that allows safe inspection and does not interfere with sails, sheets, tender handling or liferaft deployment. Follow the manufacturers' separation guidance for nearby electronics; do not guess at clearance from transmitting antennas or radar equipment.

Route cables through proper marine glands with strain relief. Avoid chafe points, hot surfaces and places where crew will stand on them. Leave a drip loop where appropriate and accessible service slack without creating snagging loops. A marine installer can assess the structure and watertight routing before you make permanent holes.

## Protect the vessel's electrical reserve

Calculate the energy demand of the entire connection, including network equipment and any inverter. Measure actual consumption if possible. For example, a system averaging 60 watts over ten hours uses 600 watt-hours before additional conversion losses. That example is a budgeting exercise, not a rating for any particular terminal.

Compare this load with usable house-battery capacity and realistic charging during your trip. Navigation lights, instruments, refrigeration and other essential systems already consume that reserve. Continuous internet use at anchor may matter more than a short period of use while the engine is charging.

Use a suitable protected circuit, correct wire sizing and a supported power supply. A nominal vessel voltage alone does not establish compatibility: charging voltage, voltage drop and startup demand also matter. Keep power supplies ventilated and dry according to their ratings. If wiring requirements are uncertain, have a qualified marine electrician design the connection.

## Plan for salt, spray and heavy weather

Weather resistance depends on the exact model and properly installed connectors. An outdoor terminal's rating does not automatically extend to every cable join, adapter, router or power supply. Check the documentation before exposing any part of the system to spray. Generic Ethernet cables can change the sealing protection of some compact terminals.

Inspect mounts, seals and connectors regularly for corrosion, looseness and chafe. Use corrosion protection only where approved by the equipment manufacturer. After salt exposure, follow the specified cleaning method; avoid pressure washing connectors or treating the antenna surface with unapproved chemicals.

Keep equipment out of predictable boarding-sea paths where possible. Heavy rain can weaken a satellite link, and an exposed installation can suffer physical damage independently of reception. Before bad weather, secure portable gear and download the information you need. Do not make a forecast update dependent on a connection remaining available throughout a squall.

## Manage data and preserve independent communications

Crew phones can consume data through photo uploads, streaming and software updates without anyone noticing. Turn off automatic cloud transfers, download entertainment ashore and check account usage regularly. Establish a shared rule for paid ocean data before departure so an unexpected bill does not become a crew dispute.

Save charts, route notes, contacts and weather information locally. Follow chart providers' update practices and use appropriate navigation tools. Check forecast timestamps: an old file on a working device can look reassuring while no longer describing current conditions.

Satellite internet is a convenience and a useful information channel, but it is not a substitute for required safety equipment, marine radio or an appropriate distress system. Keep those systems usable independently. Before a longer passage, test your offline procedures as carefully as the internet connection itself.
